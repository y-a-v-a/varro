The constraint that shapes everything

Ancient texts are long out of copyright; modern editions of them are not. Every source is therefore tiered — green ships, amber never does — and the rule is enforced in code, not documentation. The largest Latin collection in existence, Corpus Corporum's 226 million words, is declared in the manifest and deliberately never fetched, because it is non-commercial only.

That decision caps the model at roughly 25M parameters. It buys something worth more: a corpus that can actually be published, which does not currently exist.

The pipeline
1

fetch.py

manifest → data/raw

A YAML manifest declares every source with its licence, tier and format. Git sources are cloned blobless and pinned by commit; the resolved SHAs and checksums go into a lockfile that makes any build reproducible — and the licence claim auditable.

Amber sources are refused even when named explicitly. An amber entry marked include: true is a hard validation error rather than a quiet contamination.
2

extract.py

markup → text + provenance

TEI and CoNLL-U become one JSON record per work. Editorial apparatus is dropped and counted: notes, deletions, lacunae, bibliography, and <choice> resolved to the corrected reading over the manuscript error.

Language filtering needed three independent layers. Perseus ships English translations in the same directory tree, only a third of its edition records carry a language attribute, and Terence's plays hide an English introduction inside a verse line. The systematic Greek problem was not Cicero's letters but the grammarians — Priscian and Servius quoting Greek terms mid-sentence.
3

enrich.py

dating & period

A curated authority file assigns a floruit to each author and to anonymous-but-datable works. Period is derived from the date, so the two can never disagree, and every record carries how it was dated and how confidently.

147authorities curated
94.8%explicitly resolved
73.9%high confidence
Dating per work rather than per source moved 822,468 words of Perseus out of "classical" — Jerome, Ammianus, Boethius, the Historia Augusta. It cuts both ways: Gaius and Festus arrive via a late-antique library but are second century.
4

normalize.py

one orthography

The sources did not merely differ in spelling — they used opposing conventions. v as a share of u+v measured 12.9% and 11.5% in two libraries against 0.60% in the third, which writes classical uir throughout. Folding is towards u: the reverse needs morphology to know which u is consonantal.

40,672editorial marks
3,813words rejoined
233homoglyphs
Stripping combining marks in one pass caught accents, macrons, papyrological underdots and numeral overlines together — including 12,007 acute accents that turned out to be metrical stress marks in Plautus rather than orthography.
5

dedupe.py

one copy per work

Detection is by content, not title: the same work appears under different names, and the same name hides different works. Documents reduce to sketches of overlapping five-word spans, compared after folding orthography away.

60duplicates marked
886,542words
0false positives
Two relationships hide in one cluster and need opposite rules. For twins, the better-quality source wins. For containment — one text inside a fuller one — the larger must win, or the pipeline keeps a 43,000-word selection of Jerome's letters and discards the complete 290,000-word corpus. Every mark is then verified by sampling literal word-spans from the duplicate and checking they occur in the survivor.
6

build.py · tokenizer.py

corpus → tokens

Validation is held out as whole works, never random lines: a line-level split lets the model see the surrounding paragraphs of everything it is scored on. Then a byte-level BPE vocabulary is trained and measured.

1.973tokens per word
Every parameter figure in the project rested on an assumed 1.8 tokens per word. It was a good guess. The vocabulary was then chosen at 4,000 — not for compression but because a 32,000-token vocabulary would cost 8.2M parameters, larger than the model it serves, and because a small vocabulary is forced to learn Latin's stems rather than memorise its inflections.

A 3.53-million-parameter transformer, trained from random initialisation on 9.5 million tokens of classical Latin and nothing else. The floor was published before training began — a simple word-pair statistical model scores 158.8 on the same text, so anything that cannot beat it has learned nothing. Latin-0 passed it six minutes in.

Is it writing Latin, or repeating it?

The question the project turns on. A model this small, shown the same corpus nine times over, could simply be giving the text back. So generated passages are cut into windows and each is checked, word for word, against every line of training text.

Zero matches is not by itself meaningful — the window might just be too long for anything to match. So genuine Latin the model has never seen, by a real author, is scored the same way as a control.

At three words the model matches real Latin almost exactly — both reuse the corpus's common little phrases, because that is simply what the language is made of. From four words up, the model repeats the corpus less than a real Roman author does. It is not giving the text back. If anything it has not yet learned the longer set phrases that genuine Latin is full of — a limitation as much as a result.
Latin-2 — learn all of it, then specialise

The second model follows the strategy the plan proposed: read every period of Latin first, then narrow onto Rome. Fifteen hundred years of the language, then eight centuries of it.

Latin-0 and the finished Latin-2 are judged on the same held-out works against the same floor, so they can be compared directly: perplexity falls by 41%.

Two things changed at once — the model got four times larger and gained the two-phase schedule. So this shows the combination works; it does not say which half did the work. The experiment that would separate them has not been run, and saying so costs nothing.

One thing the second phase does show alone: it reached its final quality in 500 steps, where Latin-0 needed 5,000 from a random start. Shifting register inside a language you already know is far cheaper than learning the language.

And a warning sign worth reporting rather than burying. Latin-0 ended with training and held-out scores identical — no memorisation at all. Both larger runs show a gap of about 1.35×. That is memorisation beginning to appear as the model grows against a fixed amount of text, exactly as the arithmetic predicted it would. The verbatim check still comes back clean at zero, but it is now doing real work rather than confirming the obvious.
Knowing a language is not knowing things

Everything that survives of classical Latin — every speech, poem, history and letter that made it out of antiquity — is about 10 million words. That is the whole inheritance, not the part we have collected.

A frontier model
~11 trillion words · about 1,100,000× larger

The bars cannot be drawn honestly. If the first were the 4 pixels shown, Wikipedia's would run about four metres and a frontier model's roughly eleven kilometres.

Another way to feel it: a committed reader gets through perhaps five million words a year. The entire surviving written output of Roman civilisation is about two years of one person's reading.

So why did this work at all? Because language and knowledge are not the same thing, and they do not need the same amount of text. Ten million words is ample to learn how a language behaves — which endings attach to which stems, which words expect which cases, how a clause is built. It is nowhere near enough to learn what happened in the world.

Latin-0 knows how Latin works. It does not know anything about Rome. It writes well-formed words in plausible constructions and says nothing true. That is not a shortcoming of the training — it is the honest shape of what ten million words can teach.

This also means the model is not an undersized version of something bigger. Given this much text, roughly this size is the right size; a larger model would spend its extra capacity memorising rather than learning. The ceiling moves only by adding text — the fifteen centuries of Latin written after antiquity, most of which has never been digitised. That constraint is archival, not computational.

A specimen
Quid est, ut opinor, et quae sit ille, ut in rebus, quae ad modum
in rebus agrum aduentum fatum, non ut ad se non potest, ita, ut cum
aduentu resciscat; sed tamen etiam cum ab omnibus rebus, qui de rebus
aduentu rumint, pro se a me expetendo ad eum qui uenerit

Read charitably and it is Latin: ut opinor is a phrase Cicero uses, qui uenerit is a correctly formed subjunctive, and the endings agree with their neighbours far more often than not. Read closely and it falls apart — quae sit ille mismatches its genders, rumint is not a word, and no sentence finishes an argument it started.

Which is exactly the expected result. The grammar has been learned; the meaning has not.

The larger model, given the same opening, drifts into recognisably poetic vocabulary — umeros, nigra, frigidus, dulci flauae pinna are the furniture of Latin hexameter. It invents fewer non-words than the small one. It still finishes no thought it starts.

What the numbers changed

The project was planned against an assumed 10 million words of classical Latin. Measured, it is 4.64 million — and that is the figure the model has to be sized against, not the estimate.

  • Roughly 8.3M classical tokens supports a well-trained model of two to three million parameters, not the five originally planned.
  • A middle rung at 10–15M parameters would need 24–36 passes over the same data, so it was removed rather than shrunk into irrelevance.
  • The 25M-parameter target still holds — on the full corpus across all periods, which is what the two-phase plan always intended.

Nothing in that arithmetic is an estimate any more. The tokenizer settled the last one at 1.973 tokens per word, and the 25M-parameter target lands at 25.9M — as close as arithmetic gets.
