Open Latin Corpus

Turning critical editions into a training corpus

Seven stages take scholarly markup, a Wikisource dump and half a million inscriptions, and produce a licence-clean, orthographically unified corpus of Latin — with every editorial decision counted, and provenance attached to every document. Then a model is trained on it, on Latin and nothing else.

The constraint that shapes everything

Ancient texts are long out of copyright; modern editions of them are not. Every source is therefore tiered — green ships, amber never does — and the rule is enforced in code, not documentation. The largest Latin collection in existence, Corpus Corporum's 226 million words, is declared in the manifest and deliberately never fetched, because it is non-commercial only.

That decision capped the model at roughly 25M parameters. As the corpus grew past 78 million words the licence stopped being the binding constraint and the data became one — the corpus now comfortably supports the 27.49M-parameter model that was eventually trained. What the decision buys is worth more than the parameters it cost: a corpus that can actually be published, which does not currently exist.

The pipeline
1

fetch.py

manifest → data/raw

A YAML manifest declares every source with its licence, tier and format. Git sources are cloned blobless and pinned by commit; the resolved SHAs and checksums go into a lockfile that makes any build reproducible — and the licence claim auditable.

Amber sources are refused even when named explicitly. An amber entry marked include: true is a hard validation error rather than a quiet contamination.
2

extract.py

markup → text + provenance

TEI and CoNLL-U become one JSON record per work. Editorial apparatus is dropped and counted: notes, deletions, lacunae, bibliography, and <choice> resolved to the corrected reading over the manuscript error.

12,988records
53,612Greek runs stripped
75,211English words dropped
Language filtering needed three independent layers. Perseus ships English translations in the same directory tree, only a third of its edition records carry a language attribute, and Terence's plays hide an English introduction inside a verse line. The systematic Greek problem was not Cicero's letters but the grammarians — Priscian and Servius quoting Greek terms mid-sentence.
3

enrich.py

dating & period

A curated authority file assigns a floruit to each author and to anonymous-but-datable works. Period is derived from the date, so the two can never disagree, and every record carries how it was dated and how confidently.

57.0%dated
16.0Mmedieval words
217Patrologia volumes mapped
86 million words arrived from Wikisource with no author field at all, and 82.8% of the corpus fell undated. Migne's Patrologia Latina rescued most of it: its 217 volumes are ordered chronologically by author death, so a volume number dates a text without naming its author. The map is anchored on principal authors and interpolated, never linear — Migne gives Augustine sixteen volumes and the tenth century about six, so a straight line dates Jerome a century early. Undated fell from 82.8% to 43.0%.
4

normalize.py

one orthography

The sources did not merely differ in spelling — they used opposing conventions. v as a share of u+v measured 12.9% and 11.5% in two libraries against 0.60% in the third, which writes classical uir throughout. Folding is towards u: the reverse needs morphology to know which u is consonantal.

588,131editorial marks
91,505ligatures
10,373foreign words dropped
Stripping combining marks in one pass caught accents, macrons, papyrological underdots and numeral overlines together — including 12,007 acute accents that turned out to be metrical stress marks in Plautus rather than orthography.
5

dedupe.py

one copy per work

Detection is by content, not title: the same work appears under different names, and the same name hides different works. Documents reduce to sketches of overlapping five-word spans, compared after folding orthography away.

2,794duplicates marked
29.8Mwords (27.4%)
923clusters
Deduplication removes more than a quarter of everything fetched, because Latin Wikisource re-hosts a great deal of what Perseus and the patristic library already supply. Two relationships hide in one cluster and need opposite rules. For twins, the better-quality source wins. For containment — one text inside a fuller one — the larger must win, or the pipeline keeps a 43,000-word selection of Jerome's letters and discards the complete 290,000-word corpus. Every mark is then verified by sampling literal word-spans from the duplicate and checking they occur in the survivor.
6

build.py · tokenizer.py

corpus → tokens

Validation is held out as whole works, never random lines: a line-level split lets the model see the surrounding paragraphs of everything it is scored on. Then a byte-level BPE vocabulary is trained and measured.

153.0Mtokens, full corpus
9.6Mclassical
2.038tokens per word
The vocabulary is 4,000, and the reason has changed. The original argument was parameter budget — at the sizes first planned, a 32,000-token vocabulary cost more than the model it served. At 27.49M parameters that argument is gone. What decides it now is morphology, measured: of twelve forms of the verb amo, a 4k vocabulary stores none whole and splits amabamus into am · ab · amus, while a 64k vocabulary stores ten — it has simply memorised the inflections this project exists to watch a model learn.

Fertility barely moved as the corpus grew 4.6×: 2.054 tokens per word then, 2.038 now. Latin tokenises at a stable rate across fifteen centuries and wildly different registers, which means the words-to-tokens constant can be trusted for planning.

A 3.53-million-parameter transformer, trained from random initialisation on 9.5 million tokens of classical Latin and nothing else. The floor was published before training began — a simple word-pair statistical model scores 158.8 on the same text, so anything that cannot beat it has learned nothing. Latin-0 passed it six minutes in.

That 158.8 is the floor of the corpus as it stood then, at a quarter of its present size and with a different vocabulary. On today's corpus the same method scores 149.8. The two are not interchangeable, and every figure on this page is tied to the corpus it was measured on — which is the whole reason the reports carry fingerprints.

Is it writing Latin, or repeating it?

The question the project turns on. A model this small, shown the same corpus nine times over, could simply be giving the text back. So generated passages are cut into windows and each is checked, word for word, against every line of training text.

Zero matches is not by itself meaningful — the window might just be too long for anything to match. So genuine Latin the model has never seen, by a real author, is scored the same way as a control.

The short windows deserve a less comfortable reading. At three to six words this model overlaps its training text more than the control does — the reverse of what the earlier, smaller model showed. That is a bias toward the average, reaching for the commonest phrasing more often than any individual author would, rather than recall. But the control here scores unusually low on only 6,710 words, so the comparison is shaky and is reported rather than explained away. The absolute figure is the dependable one.

Only the last column compares across those rows. Each model was measured on a different corpus, through a different vocabulary, against a different floor — so the raw perplexities are scores over different text and mean nothing side by side. The project learned this the hard way: an early result was quoted against a floor computed on another corpus and flattered a model by 23%. Reports now carry a fingerprint of both the corpus and the tokenizer they were measured through, and the comparison tool refuses to print a ratio it cannot verify.

One thing the second phase shows on its own: it reached its final quality in a few hundred steps, where the first phase needed seventy-four thousand from random weights. Shifting register inside a language you already know is far cheaper than learning the language.

And a prediction that failed, in the useful direction. Memorisation was expected to grow with model size against a fixed corpus — Latin-0 showed no gap between its training and held-out scores, and the 15.90M-parameter model showed 1.34×. LATIN-XXV is 73% larger again, so it should have been worse. It measures 1.27×. More unique text per parameter means less pressure to memorise, and 4.6× more corpus bought exactly that.
What it writes now
CICERO UENERIO S.

etsi in re publica minime sunt ea quae iam inculta et prouisa sunt,
tamen non sunt ea quoquo modo dicta omnia, quia non modo a nostris
rebus, sed etiam ab externis oratio nostra inlustratur ...

CICERO UENERIO S. is the correct form for opening a Ciceronian letter — sender, recipient, and s(alutem) for "greetings". Beneath it, etsi … tamen and non modo … sed etiam are both correlative pairs opened and correctly closed across long clauses, and elsewhere the model puts a gerundive inside an indirect question. It also changes register cleanly, dropping into Plautine stage dialogue with speaker names when the context calls for it.

The smaller model, given a similar opening, produced poetic vocabulary in word-salad order. The syntax has arrived. The meaning has not, and headings are still invented outright.

Reading the output found a bug no metric had. The model generates Scr. in Formiano … Non. Mai. — which is not Cicero but a modern editor's dateline, printed above each letter in the edition and never stripped. There are 927 of them in the classical corpus. A perplexity score would never have flagged it; it took looking at the text.
Knowing a language is not knowing things

Everything that survives of classical Latin — every speech, poem, history and letter that made it out of antiquity — is about 10 million words. That is the whole inheritance, not the part we have collected.

All classical Latin
~10 million words
English Wikipedia
~4 billion words · 400× larger
A frontier model
~11 trillion words · about 1,100,000× larger

The bars cannot be drawn honestly. If the first were the 4 pixels shown, Wikipedia's would run about four metres and a frontier model's roughly eleven kilometres.

Another way to feel it: a committed reader gets through perhaps five million words a year. The entire surviving written output of Roman civilisation is about two years of one person's reading.

So why did this work at all? Because language and knowledge are not the same thing, and they do not need the same amount of text. Ten million words is ample to learn how a language behaves — which endings attach to which stems, which words expect which cases, how a clause is built. It is nowhere near enough to learn what happened in the world.

These models know how Latin works. They do not know anything about Rome. They write well-formed words in plausible constructions and say nothing true. That is not a shortcoming of the training — it is the honest shape of what the surviving language can teach, and no amount of engineering moves it.

This also means the model is not an undersized version of something bigger. Given this much text, roughly this size is the right size; a larger model would spend its extra capacity memorising rather than learning. The ceiling moves only by adding text — the fifteen centuries of Latin written after antiquity, most of which has never been digitised. That constraint is archival, not computational, which is exactly why teaching this model to read scanned books back into the corpus is the most interesting thing to do with it.

For contrast — where it started
Quid est, ut opinor, et quae sit ille, ut in rebus, quae ad modum
in rebus agrum aduentum fatum, non ut ad se non potest, ita, ut cum
aduentu resciscat; sed tamen etiam cum ab omnibus rebus, qui de rebus
aduentu rumint, pro se a me expetendo ad eum qui uenerit

That is the first model, at 3.53M parameters on the classical corpus alone — the same prompt, two rungs down the ladder. Read charitably and it is Latin: ut opinor is a phrase Cicero uses, qui uenerit is a correctly formed subjunctive, and the endings agree with their neighbours far more often than not. Read closely and it falls apart — quae sit ille mismatches its genders, rumint is not a word, and no sentence finishes an argument it started.

Which is exactly the expected result. The grammar has been learned; the meaning has not.

The middle model drifts into recognisably poetic vocabulary — umeros, nigra, frigidus, dulci flauae pinna are the furniture of Latin hexameter — and invents fewer non-words. Neither finishes a thought it starts. Set beside the Ciceronian letter above, the three together are the clearest picture of what eight times the corpus and eight times the parameters actually bought: sentence structure that holds.

What a model like this is for

It will not answer questions about Rome, and no amount of training would fix that. What it has is a precise sense of what Latin sequences are plausible — and that turns out to be the one thing several real jobs need. Point it at a text and it returns a probability. The uses follow from what you do with that number.

First, does it actually know the difference?

An instrument is worth nothing until it is tested on inputs whose answer is already known. Scored on six deliberately chosen texts, lower meaning "more like Latin":

Auditing the corpus that trained it

Every document scored, both tails read as findings. High perplexity finds text that is not Latin; low perplexity finds text carrying no information at all. Run over all 9,893 documents, 0.21% of words were flagged.

The single most surprising document in a 79-million-word Latin corpus turned out to be Lorem ipsum — web-design placeholder text, itself scrambled pseudo-Latin, sitting on a stub page titled De Oratore. Below it: a macaronic German-Latin carol, five copies of a 1580 treatise on French pronunciation, and Newton's Principia. At the other end, placeholder pages whose entire content is I - - - - - - II - - - -, and a navigation index repeating one link title for 2,206 words.

The detector needed a second signal to be safe. Flagging on low perplexity alone condemned 194 documents; requiring low vocabulary variety as well cut that to 24. Eighty-seven per cent of the first list was legitimately formulaic Latin — epigraphy, liturgy, legal boilerplate — which is predictable precisely because it is real. Roman brick stamps were mass-produced bearing identical text, so a chunk of them repeats a phrase hundreds of times and is not a defect at all. The tool flags it; deciding which it is remains a job for a reader.

Three more the same number supports

  • Correcting OCR. Fifteen centuries of Latin sit un-digitised in scanned books, and reading them by machine is how this corpus grows. Given several candidate readings of a smudged line, the model says which is likelier Latin — needing no knowledge of the world, only a sense of the language. The corpus trains the model; the model improves the OCR; the OCR grows the corpus.
  • Dating anonymous texts. The model was trained with era labels, so the same passage can be scored as classical, late antique, medieval or neo-Latin and the best fit reported. 43% of the corpus currently has no date at all.
  • Restoring damaged text. Inscriptions and manuscripts come with holes in them. Proposing what filled a gap is real scholarly work, and formulaic epigraphy — of which this corpus holds half a million pieces — is where a small model is strongest.
What it is not is a grammar checker. It reports improbability, not error, and cannot tell the two apart: a genuine archaism and a scanning mistake are both surprising. For agreement and case government, a morphological analyser does the real work. This is a screen, not an authority.
Where this stands

Five of nine freely-licensed sources are in. The corpus is built, deduplicated, dated and normalised; the tokenizer is settled on evidence; the evaluation apparatus was written before any model existed; and the flagship model is trained, measured and audited.

It will not know facts, and the section above explains why that is a property of the surviving language rather than of the engineering. A demonstration that implied otherwise would be a worse result, not a better one.
Glossary

The measurements on this page in plain terms.

TermWhat it means here
Perplexity (ppl) How surprised the model is by text it is shown, as an average over every word. Read it as "how many equally-likely options was it choosing between at each step" — 30 means roughly a thirty-way guess, 300 means it is lost. Lower is better. It measures prediction, never understanding.
Floor (baseline) The score of a deliberately stupid method — here, one that only knows which word tends to follow which. Anything that cannot beat it has learned nothing. Stating the floor before training is what stops a mediocre result being described as a good one afterwards.
Token The unit the model actually reads. Not quite a word: common words are one token, rarer ones split into pieces, so amabamus becomes am · ab · amus. Latin runs about 2.04 tokens per word.
Tokenizer / vocabulary The fixed list of pieces text is cut into, learned from the corpus. A small vocabulary (4,000 here) forces the model to recognise stems and endings; a large one lets it store whole inflected forms and learn nothing from them.
Parameters The adjustable numbers inside the model — its capacity to store what it learns. LATIN-XXV has 27.49 million. Frontier models have hundreds of billions.
Epoch One complete pass through the training text. Four passes is about the point where re-reading the same material stops teaching much, which is why the size of the corpus, not the speed of the computer, decides how large a model can usefully be.
Held-out / validation Text deliberately withheld from training, kept to score the model on. Here it is held out by whole author — holding out one work by Augustine while training on thirty others would test recognition, not generalisation.
Overfitting / train-val gap The model doing better on text it studied than on text it has not seen. The ratio between the two is the warning light: 1.00× is no gap at all, LATIN-XXV shows 1.27×. A widening gap means it is starting to memorise rather than generalise.
Memorisation check Cutting generated text into runs of words and searching the entire training corpus for each one, literally. It cannot report a false positive. Zero at twelve words means the model composed the passage rather than reproducing one.
Pretraining The first and longest stage: learning the language by predicting the next token, over and over, with no instructions and no supervision. Everything on this page is pretraining.
Fine-tuning (SFT) A short second training pass that changes behaviour rather than knowledge — teaching a model to take conversational turns, for instance. It cannot add facts the pretraining never contained.
From scratch / random initialisation Starting from meaningless numbers rather than adapting somebody else's trained model. It is what makes "trained on Latin alone" a literal claim: there is no English anywhere underneath.
Context window How much text the model can consider at once — 512 tokens here, a few hundred words. It has no memory beyond that; each passage is read afresh.
Checkpoint A saved copy of the model mid-training, so a run of many hours can be resumed rather than restarted.
Deduplication Finding the same work arriving from several libraries. It matters because repetition is a dial the training deliberately controls; accidental duplicates corrupt it. Here it removed 27.4% of everything fetched.
