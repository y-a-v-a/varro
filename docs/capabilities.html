Varro · what the model can and cannot do

LATIN-XXV-2, measured

Every number here comes from a generated report, not a transcription. Where the model loses to something simpler, that is on the page too — it is the most useful thing we found out.

The short version
It can

Tell Latin from not-Latin by an order of magnitude. Write grammatical Latin without reciting its training text. Find damaged and foreign passages in a corpus of 78 million words.

It cannot

Date a text — counting words beats it by 31 points. Know anything about the world. Explain any judgement it makes; it reports that a passage is improbable, never why.

Capability 1 · telling Latin from not-Latin

The separation is wide, and one row explains the model

Real Latin scores 41.4; the same words shuffled score 106.4. Identical vocabulary, identical word frequencies, different order — and the model is 2.6× more surprised. A frequency table would score those two texts exactly the same. That gap is the whole of what this model adds over counting words, and it predicts where it will be useful and where it will not.
Capability 2 · composing rather than reciting

Cleaning the corpus halved what the model repeats

Nothing survives at eight words, in any model this project has trained. The second generation reaches zero at five, where the first was still reproducing text at six — and from five words up it now sits below genuine unseen Latin, which the first generation never managed. The only thing that changed between them was the corpus: 930 repeated editorial datelines and a heap of degenerate navigation text were removed.
Capability 3 · dating a text

Where it loses, and to what

A bag of words beats the model by 31 points. What separates medieval Latin from classical is largely which words appear, not the order they come in — so a frequency table captures the whole signal and the model adds nothing. This is the same finding as the shuffled-text row above, seen from the other side.
Late antique Latin is where the model breaks down — 20 records called classical, 20 late, 27 medieval, which is very close to guessing. That is not unreasonable of it: late antique Latin genuinely sits between the two, and the model has no sharper notion of it than "somewhere in the middle". Classical and medieval it separates tolerably, at 76% and 67% recall.
Training

Two generations, the same recipe, 25 hours each

The second generation is consistently about 2% worse on perplexity and better on every behavioural measure. The 930 editorial datelines it no longer contains were formulaic and therefore easy to predict; removing them cost perplexity and bought a model that recites less and no longer generates editorial furniture. Perplexity is a poor instrument for judging corpus quality, because junk is predictable.
