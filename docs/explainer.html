Varro · what the trained models actually are

Two models on disk

A trained language model is not a program. It is a file of numbers, plus a small dictionary telling you what the numbers are about. Everything else in the folder is bookkeeping.

There are now two of them, and the difference between them is not their quality. It is their licence.

Why two

The difference is the licence, not the quality

Varro assembles Latin from sources with incompatible terms. Some may be redistributed; some may be trained on but not passed along. Rather than average that into one uncomfortable answer, the corpus is built twice, and each corpus produces its own model.

ModelNumbersTrained onLicence
latin-vii 7,278,912 Classical Latin only — 5,055,913 words from 5 freely licensed sources CC BY-SA · publishable
latin-lii 52,066,560 All the Latin there is — 134,566,581 words from 11 sources NonCommercial · research only

The larger model is better at Latin by a wide margin: it scores 27.3 on classical text against LATIN-VII's 70.1, on the same held-out passages through the same vocabulary. But it read material that cannot be redistributed, so its weights cannot be either. LATIN-VII is the weaker model and the only one you can hand to someone.

The gap is not about model size. Both models are sized correctly for the text they saw — about twenty tokens per parameter, the ratio that balances the two. LATIN-VII simply had 10.7 million tokens to learn from and LATIN-LII had 263.6 million. Doubling the parameters of a small model bought 6%; giving a model twenty-five times more Latin bought 61%. At this scale data matters far more than sizing.
The inventory

Six files, of which two are the model

Each training run writes six files. Only the first two are needed to use the model — the rest let you resume an interrupted run, or establish where the model came from. If you copied just those two onto another machine, it would work. The sizes below are LATIN-LII's; LATIN-VII's are the same files, roughly seven times smaller.

FileSizeWhat it holdsNeeded to run?
latin-lii-latinitas.safetensors 198.6 MB The 52,066,560 learned numbers. This is the model. Essential
latin-bpe-4000.json 250 KB The 4,000 word-pieces the numbers are indexed by. Essential
latin-lii-latinitas.opt.safetensors 397.3 MB The optimiser's working notes — twice the size of the model, and discardable. Resume only
latin-lii-latinitas.state.json 2 KB Which step training reached, and the loss at each checkpoint. Resume only
train-latin-lii-latinitas.json 4 KB What it was trained on, through which vocabulary, and how it scored. Provenance
latin-lii.yaml 2 KB The recipe — enough to train it again from scratch. Provenance
Why the files say latin-lii-latinitas and the model is called LATIN-LII. Two-phase recipes here name phase one <run>-latinitas and phase two just <run>. LATIN-LII has no phase two: the classical-narrowing phase was run, it made the model worse, and it was dropped from the recipe. Those weights are kept — deleting a measurement is worse than labelling one — but under latin-lii-romanitas, so there is no bare latin-lii checkpoint to load by mistake. Every number on this page belongs to latin-lii-latinitas.
The optimiser file is twice the size of the model itself, which surprises people. While training, the optimiser keeps two running averages for every single weight — so three numbers are in flight for each one that survives. When training ends, its notes are finished with.
Opening the first file

The weights are 104 tables of numbers

Inside latin-lii-latinitas.safetensors there is no code and no text — just named grids of decimals. Here are four of the hundred and four, at their real sizes: 

embed.weight                      (4000, 640)   float32
pos.weight                        ( 512, 640)   float32
blocks.6.fc1.weight               (2560, 640)   float32
blocks.6.attn.value_proj.weight   ( 640, 640)   float32

embed.weight is the one worth understanding first. It has one row per word-piece — 4,000 rows — and each row is 640 numbers long. That row is what the model knows about that piece. Row 2247 belongs to the piece Gall, and begins: 

-0.0133  -0.0136  -0.0814  -0.0976  -0.0505  0.0753  0.0684  0.0084  …

No single number in that row means anything on its own. Nothing in the file says “Gaul” or “noun” or “place”. The meaning is entirely in how the 640 numbers sit relative to every other row — which is why you cannot open a model and read it, and why the only way to find out what it learned is to run it and look at what comes out.

The remaining hundred-odd tables are the layers that transform those rows in context — ten repeating blocks, each with an attention part that lets a word look at the words around it, and a larger part that does the actual reshaping. blocks.6.fc1.weight is one of the latter, in the seventh of the ten blocks. LATIN-VII has the same shape in miniature: six blocks, 288 numbers per row, 64 tables in all.

Opening the second file

The vocabulary is a list of 4,000 pieces

The model cannot read letters. Before any Latin reaches it, the text is cut into pieces from a fixed list, and each piece is replaced by its position in that list. Watching one word go through is the clearest way to see it:

Latin
amabamus  — “we were loving”
Cut into pieces
am · ab · amus
Looked up
527 · 344 · 739
What the model sees
three rows of 640 numbers

Notice the split: am is the stem, ab marks the imperfect tense, amus marks “we”. The vocabulary is small on purpose — at 4,000 pieces the model is forced to learn Latin's endings, because it cannot afford to store every inflected form whole. A 64,000-piece vocabulary stores amabamus as a single entry and learns nothing from it.

Six entries in the list are not Latin at all. <|endoftext|> marks where one document ends, and four era markers — <CLASSICAL>, <LATE>, <MEDIEVAL>, <NEOLATIN> — were placed in front of each document during training, so the model can be asked for one period rather than another.

Why both

The small file is not the optional one

weights
198.6 MB
vocabulary
250 KB · 0.13%

The vocabulary is an eighth of one per cent of the model's size, and the model is worthless without it. The weights are indexed by position in that list: row 2247 means Gall only because the list says entry 2247 is Gall. Hand the model a different list and every row points at the wrong thing.

This is not hypothetical, and it has now happened twice. On 20 August a corpus rebuild retrained the vocabulary in place and overwrote the previous model's list; the weights were untouched and completely unusable. It would not have failed — it would have produced fluent, confident, meaningless Latin. That file was recovered by rebuilding the corpus that produced it and retraining the vocabulary, which came back byte for byte identical.
On 1 September the older LATIN-XXV-2 was scored against today's corpus by mistake, through today's vocabulary rather than the one it was trained on. It reported a perplexity of 3,740 — against its true 28.3. Nothing crashed; the number was simply meaningless. Vocabularies are archived by content (latin-bpe-4000.f64b7246a477af75.json), never overwritten, and every model records the fingerprint of the one it read.
What it is not

Three things that are not in the folder

The corpus is not in there. LATIN-LII learned from 865 MB of Latin and is 198.6 MB — under a quarter of the size, and not a compressed copy. Of 3,411 twelve-word passages it generated, none appear in its training text, and its longest verbatim run is zero words. LATIN-VII, which read every classical passage fourteen times over, produced 3,476 and also matched on none.

The control matters more than either figure: genuinely unseen Latin, held out of training entirely, overlaps the training text at 6.0% of twelve-word windows, because scripture and liturgy and formula recur across centuries. From four words upward both models match the training text less often than real Latin does. They absorbed how Latin behaves, not the sentences they read — and that is measured rather than assumed, because it is what allows a model trained on non-redistributable text to be described honestly.

There is no knowledge in there. It writes grammatical Latin about nothing. Asked for classical prose it produced a sentence placing the town of Munda near the Nile — Munda is in Spain. All surviving Latin, across fifteen centuries, is roughly two years of one committed reader's intake; that is enough to learn a language and nowhere near enough to learn the world.

There is no program in there. The file holds only numbers. Something has to load them, feed them text, and read off the answer — that is what src/sample.py and src/score.py do. The same weights run under any code that knows their shape.

Using it

Two things it can do today

Write Latin

python src/sample.py --name latin-lii-latinitas --n 5 --period CLASSICAL

Give it a starting point and it continues. Conditioned on <CLASSICAL> it writes like a Roman historian; on <MEDIEVAL>, like a monastic chronicler.

Judge Latin

python src/score.py --name latin-lii-latinitas FILE
python src/score.py --name latin-lii-latinitas --period-fit FILE

Run backwards, the model reports how surprising a piece of text is. That number sorts real Latin from damaged Latin from text that is not Latin at all — and scoring the same passage under each era marker suggests which century it belongs to.

Both routes check the vocabulary's fingerprint against the one recorded when the model was trained, and refuse to run on a mismatch rather than quietly producing nonsense.
Provenance

What the model can prove about itself

The two small bookkeeping files are what separate a model you can publish from a file of numbers somebody hands you. Between them they record:

  • the exact corpus build it saw — for LATIN-LII, 11,706 documents and 134,566,581 words, every source licensed and named;
  • the fingerprint of the vocabulary it was trained through;
  • the recipe that produced it, stated in passes over the corpus rather than step counts, so re-running it on a larger corpus still means the same thing;
  • its score, against a deliberately simple method measured on the same text through the same vocabulary.
ModelIts scoreSimple methodBetter by
latin-lii21.3185.98.73×
latin-vii70.1185.72.65×

Those comparisons are only meaningful because each pair was measured on the same text through the same vocabulary — and because the simple method was computed on each model's own corpus rather than borrowed from the other. LATIN-LII's corpus is genuinely harder than LATIN-VII's: its baseline is 185.9 against 185.7, and the classical slice is the hardest of all at 201.2, being the least formulaic Latin in the collection.

One number in this project is now known to be wrong in the other direction. LATIN-LII was trained in two phases, the second narrowing onto classical Latin, and that phase made it worse at classical — 27.3 before, 30.2 after. The same phase improves the smaller LATIN-XXV-2 (34.8 → 28.3). The narrowing phase has a capacity ceiling and 52 million parameters is past it: with only 10.7 million classical tokens to re-read, the model memorises rather than learns. The published LATIN-LII is the first phase alone.
