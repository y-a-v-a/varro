Can the model mend a bad scan?

Old Latin books reach us through OCR — software that reads a page image and guesses the letters. It guesses wrong often enough (loue for Ioue, femen for semen) that a lot of digitised Latin is quietly damaged. The question here is whether LATIN-LII, which has read 134 million words of Latin, can put some of that right without breaking the text that was already correct.

Short answer: yes, measurably better than simple word counting — but on real scans it repairs about a quarter of the damage, and every number below is from the practice set. The final exam has not been sat yet.

The question

Does reading the context beat counting words?

Nearly anything fixes cu1tu. The interesting claim is narrower: the model should earn its keep where word order carries the answer, and lose wherever a frequency table already knows it. So the benchmark gives a plain word-frequency repairer every advantage except order — the same list of candidate corrections, the same model of how OCR goes wrong, the same rule for when to change a word — and measures the gap. The systems differ in one term only: how likely each candidate looks.

SystemHow it judges a candidate word
identityChanges nothing. A sanity check: must score zero everywhere.
unigramHow common is this word in Latin? The yardstick.
liiLATIN-LII reads the words before and asks how likely this word, and the words after it, are.
fimA version of LATIN-LII given 3.6 h of extra training to see the text on both sides of a gap at once (“fill in the middle”).

Harm is fixed in advance: a system may break at most 2 correct words in every 1,000. That is deliberately strict — a proofreader who introduced one error per two pages would be let go — and every result below is tuned to sit under it.

Words used on this page

A small glossary

recall ↑
Of the words the scan got wrong, the share the system put right.
precision ↑
Of the changes the system made, the share that were correct.
broken / 1,000 ↓
Correct words the system damaged, per thousand it left standing. The harm limit is 2.
non-word error
The OCR produced something that isn't Latin (Almafalutiferam). Easy to spot.
real-word error
The OCR produced a different real word (femen/semen from a long-s). Only context can catch it.
proposer
The step that lists possible corrections for a word. If the right one isn't on the list, no judge can pick it.
ceiling
Recall if a perfect judge always picked the right candidate from the list. The limit the proposer sets.
Tier 1 / Tier 2
Tier 1: clean text with damage simulated by a noise table. Tier 2: real OCR output from scans on Wikimedia Commons, against the checked Wikisource transcription of the same page.
dev / test
Practice set, used for all tuning; exam set, run once at the end so the tuning can't have leaked into it.
Results · practice set, all under the harm limit

Context wins clearly on invented damage, modestly on real scans

On simulated damage the model repairs two-thirds of the errors; the frequency count under half. On real scans everything drops by roughly 2.5×, and the model's lead narrows to 6–8 points. Real OCR is harder than any noise table anyone writes by hand, which is why only Tier 2 figures should ever be quoted as “OCR performance”.

The awkward column is real-word damage on real scans. There the plain frequency count beats the model, 18.9% to 13.3%. The model gains all its lead on non-words (34% vs 20%). That is the opposite of the pitch — “context catches what a dictionary can't” — and it is mostly long-s: femen is a real word, so the model needs strong evidence to overrule it, and the harm limit rarely lets it. The model is better at finding damage, though: it ranks damaged words above sound ones far more reliably (average precision 62.8% vs 42.7% ↑).
Where the other 73% goes

The limit is now the caution, not the candidates

Every damaged word in Tier 2, for the best system (fim), ends up in one of four places. Until 18 September the widest band was “right answer never offered”; a rebuilt proposer raised the ceiling from 48% to 57%. But the extra candidates mostly landed in the second band: offered, and declined.

27% repaired
24% right answer offered, system declined to change the word (1,756 words)
~6% offered, but a wrong candidate won or the word was otherwise missed
43% right answer never offered — out of reach for any judge
Derived from the proposer ceiling (57.4%), recall (27.3%) and the 1,756 declined words in HANDOVER.md; the middle split is approximate.

Why so cautious? Because on real scans the harm budget is being spent on errors in the answer key. Wikisource's “proofread” pages still contain typos. Of the 48 words fim “broke” in 32,500, about 35 look like cases where the model was right and the transcription was wrong (cceperis → coeperis, fyncerus, oetatem). Genuine harm is closer to 10 (muticus → musicus, benefitio → beneficio). To stay under 2 per 1,000 anyway, the tuned rule demands so much evidence that it leaves 1,756 correctly-offered fixes on the table.

This is the most important thing on the page. Relaxed to a lower margin, the same system reaches 40.5% recall while breaking 1.02 per 1,000 of the words the lexicon recognises. That is a bigger gain than all the proposer work combined — and it costs a decision, not engineering. But “~35 of 48 were the key's fault” is an eyeball read of one run, not a measurement.
What was learned

Nine findings, in the order they arrived

  • Safety comes from the decision rule, not the model. Picking the most likely candidate broke 6 words per 1,000; a rule that asks for more evidence the riskier the change brings it to ~1.4, at a cost of ~10 points of recall.
  • Simulated damage flatters everyone by about 2.5×. Tier 1 is useful for development, useless as a headline.
  • The candidate list was the first wall. Rebuilt: keeps capitals, searches up to 3 edits away, splits run-together words, handles symbols. Ceiling 48 → 57%, still 0.7 s per passage.
  • …but the harm limit swallowed that gain on real scans (27.1 → 27.3%), while Tier 1 showed it in full (60 → 67%).
  • The harm budget on real scans is mostly the answer key's typos (above).
  • Fill-in-the-middle training worked as a judge, not as a writer. +2 points recall on both tiers without hurting ordinary reading. But asked to generate the missing word, it ignores the text after the gap and never knows when to stop, so its suggestions add almost nothing (ceiling +0.1).
  • The model loses to word counts on real-word errors in real scans and wins by 10–17 points on non-words.
  • Page-level clues (“this word appears correctly elsewhere on the page”) are built but do nothing, because passages are only 60 words long.
  • More right-hand context doesn't help — 12 words is no better than the default.
Next steps · honestly rated

What is worth doing, and what mostly isn't

Rated by what each step would change about what you know or can do — not by how interesting it is. Roughly in the order they should happen.

1

Commit the work

Do nowminutes

Everything since 17 September — the Tier 2 benchmark, the new proposer, the tuner, the FIM training script and 52 tests — is uncommitted. Nothing here is a research question; it's a week of work sitting in one working tree.

2

Decide what “harm” means on real scans

High valuea decision, plus an afternoon if you adjudicate

Three choices: keep counting every disagreement with the key as harm; apply the 2‰ limit only to words the lexicon knows and report the rest separately as an upper bound; or look at the ~50 “broken” words per run by hand and mark which are the key's fault.

Honest take: this is worth more recall (plausibly 27% → ~40%) than any remaining engineering. The trap is choosing the definition because it flatters the system. Hand-adjudicating one run is the only option that turns “about 35 of 48” into a fact; I would do that first and let it decide between the other two. Settle it before step 3, never after.

3

Freeze the rules, sit the exam once, fix the README

High value~1–2 h compute

Run --split test on both tiers with the tuned rules, then update the ocr/README.md tables, which still show the numbers from before the proposer rebuild.

Honest take: until this happens, no number on this page can be quoted outside the repo. It is cheap. It is also one-way — after it, the hypotheses are frozen — which is exactly why step 2 goes first.

4

Decide what the repair is for

High valuea conversation

The sub-project has answered its research question: context helps, at equal harm. It has not answered whether to use it. The obvious use is cleaning the OCR-derived text in the training corpus (5.85% of corpus-b, from CSEL) before the next model. At today's numbers that removes about one in six character errors from that slice.

Honest take: that is a real improvement to a small slice of the corpus, and nobody knows yet whether it would change the next model at all. If the answer to “what for?” is “the corpus”, step 5 (the ablation) becomes the test of whether any of this matters; if it's “a tool people use on their own scans”, steps 6 and 9 matter more. Worth deciding before investing further.

5

Ablation: train without the OCR-derived text

Medium valueone retrain

Retrain with the CSEL OCR text left out and rerun both tiers. It tells you two things: whether the model has learned some OCR mistakes as Latin (which would make it reluctant to fix them), and whether that 5.85% of noisy text hurts the model in general.

Honest take: good hygiene and a credible answer to an obvious reviewer question. If removing the text changes nothing, the case for cleaning the corpus weakens considerably — which is itself worth knowing.

6

Speed: score several words per model call

Conditionala day of engineering

The model is currently asked about one word at a time; recording decisions for 650 passages takes ~30 minutes.

Honest take: changes no result. Worth it only if you mean to run repair over a real corpus or iterate on the rule many more times. Otherwise it's comfort.

7

Whole-page context

Speculativere-cut Tier 2 by page · re-lock

Not in the handover's list, but it follows from findings 7 and 8. The model's weakest spot is long-s (femen/semen), and the way a human proofreader settles those is by seeing the word printed correctly elsewhere on the page. The code for that clue exists; the 60-word passages starve it.

Honest take: the only idea on this list aimed at the model's actual weakness. Unproven, and re-cutting Tier 2 means re-locking it, so it must happen before the exam in step 3 or become a second benchmark.

8

Replace Tier 1's noise table with the one learned from real scans

Low valuere-locks Tier 1

Honest take: it would make Tier 1 more realistic, and change every Tier 1 number, in order to improve a benchmark that is no longer the judge. Simpler to state plainly that Tier 1 is a development check and quote only Tier 2.

9

Retrain fill-in-the-middle so it can generate

Low value now4+ h GPU · uncertain

Weight the training on the gap and on a proper “fill ends here” signal, so the model can propose words the lexicon doesn't contain — mostly names, about 30% of what is never offered.

Honest take: it raises the ceiling, and the ceiling is not what's binding — the system already declines a quarter of all damage it was offered. At best ~13 points of ceiling, of which the harm limit would let through a fraction. The first attempt failed at exactly this. Revisit after step 2 changes the picture.

10

Option 2: a model that rewrites the whole passage

Not yetlarge

Honest take: the approach most likely to look impressive in a demo and most likely to damage text silently — changing correct Latin into other plausible Latin that no one catches. Only with Tier 2 as the judge, and only once the cheaper routes are exhausted.

One thing this project hasn't measured: how much of the damage a modern OCR engine would avoid by simply re-reading the same scans. The Commons text layers are whatever OCR produced them, often years ago. If a fresh OCR pass removes more errors than repair does, that changes the value of every step above — and the Tier 2 benchmark could answer the question in an afternoon.
In one paragraph

Where it stands

The evaluation is sound and the scientific answer is in: reading context repairs more scanned Latin than counting words, at the same strict harm limit, and the fill-in-the-middle variant is the best of the three. On real scans that means about a quarter of the damage fixed and one or two correct words broken per thousand. The biggest remaining lever is not the model but how harm is counted against an imperfect answer key. Commit, settle that, sit the exam once — and then decide whether the goal is a cleaner corpus or a tool, because the right next investment depends on which.
