Varro · OCR sub-project · where it stands

Can the model mend a bad scan?

Old Latin books reach us through OCR — software that reads a page image and guesses the letters. It guesses wrong often enough (loue for Ioue, femen for semen) that a lot of digitised Latin is quietly damaged. The question here is whether LATIN-LII, which has read 134 million words of Latin, can put some of that right without breaking the text that was already correct — and, since 23 September, whether that makes archive.org's scanned Latin good enough to train the next model on.

Short answer: the model repairs better than simple word counting, but only a fifth to a quarter of the damage on real scans. For archive.org the bigger levers turned out to be elsewhere: getting a fresh OCR reading of a book halves its errors where repair takes off a seventh, and almost half of what we looked at is text the corpus already has.

A small glossary

characters wrong ↓
Also “character error rate” or CER: the share of letters, spaces and punctuation that differ from the proofread text. 5% means about one slip every 20 characters — roughly two per line.
words wrong ↓
The share of words not exactly right, punctuation included. Always much higher than characters wrong, because one bad letter spoils a whole word.
recall ↑
Of the words the scan got wrong, the share the system put right.
precision ↑
Of the changes the system made, the share that were correct.
broken / 1,000 ↓
Correct words the system damaged, per thousand it left standing. The harm limit is 2.
non-word error
The OCR produced something that isn't Latin (Almafalutiferam). Easy to spot.
real-word error
The OCR produced a different real word (femen/semen from a long-s). Only context can catch it.
proposer
The step that lists possible corrections for a word. If the right one isn't on the list, no judge can pick it.
ceiling
Recall if a perfect judge always picked the right candidate from the list. The limit the proposer sets.
re-read
The same page images put through OCR again, usually by a newer engine. archive.org has done this to many older scans with Tesseract.
Tier 1 / 2 / 3
Tier 1: clean text with damage simulated by a noise table. Tier 2: real OCR from scans on Wikimedia Commons, against the proofread Wikisource transcription of the same page. Tier 3 (new): archive.org's OCR of the same kind of pages.
dev / test
Practice set, used for all tuning; exam set, run once at the end so the tuning can't have leaked into it.
already in the corpus
A passage shares a run of 8 consecutive words with the training text — the model has probably read it, in this or another edition.
Part 1

The repair benchmark

The question

Does reading the context beat counting words?

Nearly anything fixes cu1tu. The interesting claim is narrower: the model should earn its keep where word order carries the answer, and lose wherever a frequency table already knows it. So the benchmark gives a plain word-frequency repairer every advantage except order — the same list of candidate corrections, the same model of how OCR goes wrong, the same rule for when to change a word — and measures the gap. The systems differ in one term only: how likely each candidate looks.

SystemHow it judges a candidate word
identityChanges nothing. A sanity check: must score zero everywhere.
unigramHow common is this word in Latin? The yardstick.
liiLATIN-LII reads the words before and asks how likely this word, and the words after it, are.
fimA version of LATIN-LII given 3.6 h of extra training to see the text on both sides of a gap at once (“fill in the middle”).

Harm is fixed in advance: a system may break at most 2 correct words in every 1,000. That is deliberately strict — a proofreader who introduced one error per two pages would be let go — and every result below is tuned to sit under it.

Results · practice set, all under the harm limit

Context wins clearly on invented damage, modestly on real scans

On simulated damage the model repairs two-thirds of the errors; the frequency count under half. On real scans everything drops by roughly 2.5×, and the model's lead narrows to 6–8 points. Real OCR is harder than any noise table anyone writes by hand, which is why only real-scan figures should ever be quoted as “OCR performance”.

The awkward column is real-word damage on real scans. There the plain frequency count beats the model, 18.9% to 13.3%. The model gains all its lead on non-words (34% vs 20%). That is the opposite of the pitch — “context catches what a dictionary can't” — and it is mostly long-s: femen is a real word, so the model needs strong evidence to overrule it, and the harm limit rarely lets it. The model is better at finding damage, though: it ranks damaged words above sound ones far more reliably (average precision 62.8% vs 42.7% ↑).
Where the other 73% goes

The limit is now the caution, not the candidates

Every damaged word in Tier 2, for the best system (fim), ends up in one of four places. Until 18 September the widest band was “right answer never offered”; a rebuilt proposer raised the ceiling from 48% to 57%. But the extra candidates mostly landed in the second band: offered, and declined.

27% repaired
24% right answer offered, system declined to change the word (1,756 words)
~6% offered, but a wrong candidate won or the word was otherwise missed
43% right answer never offered — out of reach for any judge
Derived from the proposer ceiling (57.4%), recall (27.3%) and the 1,756 declined words in HANDOVER.md; the middle split is approximate.

Why so cautious? Because on real scans the harm budget is being spent on errors in the answer key. Wikisource's “proofread” pages still contain typos. Of the 48 words fim “broke” in 32,500, about 35 look like cases where the model was right and the transcription was wrong (cceperis → coeperis, fyncerus, oetatem). Genuine harm is closer to 10 (muticus → musicus, benefitio → beneficio). To stay under 2 per 1,000 anyway, the tuned rule demands so much evidence that it leaves 1,756 correctly-offered fixes on the table.

Still the biggest lever inside the repairer. Relaxed to a lower margin, the same system reaches 40.5% recall while breaking 1.02 per 1,000 of the words the lexicon recognises. That is a bigger gain than all the proposer work combined — and it costs a decision, not engineering. But “~35 of 48 were the key's fault” is an eyeball read of one run, not a measurement.
What was learned

Nine findings about the repairer

  • Safety comes from the decision rule, not the model. Picking the most likely candidate broke 6 words per 1,000; a rule that asks for more evidence the riskier the change brings it to ~1.4, at a cost of ~10 points of recall.
  • Simulated damage flatters everyone by about 2.5×. Tier 1 is useful for development, useless as a headline.
  • The candidate list was the first wall. Rebuilt: keeps capitals, searches up to 3 edits away, splits run-together words, handles symbols. Ceiling 48 → 57%, still 0.7 s per passage.
  • …but the harm limit swallowed that gain on real scans (27.1 → 27.3%), while Tier 1 showed it in full (60 → 67%).
  • The harm budget on real scans is mostly the answer key's typos (above).
  • Fill-in-the-middle training worked as a judge, not as a writer. +2 points recall on both tiers without hurting ordinary reading. But asked to generate the missing word, it ignores the text after the gap and never knows when to stop, so its suggestions add almost nothing (ceiling +0.1).
  • The model loses to word counts on real-word errors in real scans and wins by 10–17 points on non-words.
  • Page-level clues (“this word appears correctly elsewhere on the page”) are built but do nothing, because passages are only 60 words long.
  • More right-hand context doesn't help — 12 words is no better than the default.
Part 2 · added 23–24 September

archive.org: can its Latin be made fit for the corpus?

The goal behind the whole sub-project: download OCR text from archive.org, correct it well enough, and add it to the training corpus. Before any of that is worth building, four things need knowing — does the Tier 2 result hold for archive.org's text; does a fresh OCR reading beat repair; how much of it is new to the corpus; and how clean does it end up.

Finding 1

Tier 2 was already measuring archive.org

Many of the scans Wikisource volunteers proofread were uploaded to Wikimedia Commons from archive.org. The Commons file pages of the Tier 2 books cite 66 archive.org items. For each, fifteen random pages were checked: does an 8-word run from the Commons OCR appear, letter for letter, in archive.org's current text?

For books from before about 1700, neither helps much.

Analysis

Is archive.org's Latin “corrected enough” for the corpus?

For some books, yes; for archive.org as a whole, not by repair alone. The evidence points to a pipeline in which the model is the last and smallest step:

1 · Deduplicate
Drop what the corpus already has. Cheap (no model), and removes about half of everything in this sample — nearly all of the classical texts.
2 · Best reading
Use archive.org's current OCR, and get a fresh reading where it is still the old ABBYY output. For post-1850 print this is the largest single gain measured here.
3 · Repair
Run fim under the Tier 2 rules: about a seventh of the remaining character errors, one word in five of the damaged ones, about one correct word broken per 1,000.
4 · Gate
Admit a page or book only if its estimated error rate is under a threshold. Not built yet — and it decides more about corpus quality than steps 2 and 3 together.
5 · Tag & cap
Mark the text ocr-repaired and cap its share, as build.py already caps the OCR-derived CSEL text.

Three honest caveats. First, much of what is new is early-modern neo-Latin in old print, exactly where re-reading and repair help least; the books that come out clean are mostly the ones that went in clean — which, usefully, includes much of the modern teaching Latin that is also new. Second, the worry that the model trains on its own corrections is real but small: it changes about one correct word per 1,000 into something it finds plausible, while leaving four-fifths of the OCR damage in place — the leftover OCR noise matters far more than the model's fingerprints. Third, speed: Tier 3 took about 57 minutes per model for 728 passages (~4.7 s per 60 words), so a 100,000-word book would take about two hours. Batching the model calls is required before any real harvest.

Estimate each page's error rate without a proofread copy — from the share of words the model flags as suspicious (it ranks damage well: average precision ~62%), the share it doesn't know, and the OCR engine's own confidence where archive.org provides it. Tier 3 has the truth to check the estimate against.

Honest take: this decides what enters the corpus, so it decides corpus quality more than any repair improvement could. The bar it must clear: telling a 1% page from a 5% page reliably. Unknown whether it can.

2

Size the harvest before building for it

High value · newa day · no model

Search archive.org's ~180,000 Latin items (1450–1930), download plain text for a sample of a few hundred, and measure three things per item: how much is already in the corpus, which OCR engine read it, and its publication date.

Honest take: cheap, and it answers the question everything else depends on — how many words of genuinely new, recent-enough print are there? If the answer is small, most of the steps below aren't worth it.

3

Decide what “harm” means on real scans

High valuea decision, plus an afternoon if you adjudicate

Three choices: keep counting every disagreement with the key as harm; apply the 2‰ limit only to words the lexicon knows and report the rest separately as an upper bound; or look at the ~50 “broken” words per run by hand and mark which are the key's fault.

Honest take: still the biggest lever inside the repairer (plausibly 27% → ~40% recall). The trap is choosing the definition because it flatters the system. Hand-adjudicate one run first. Settle it before step 4, never after.

Freeze the rules, sit the exam once, fix the README

High value~1–2 h compute

Run --split test on Tiers 1 and 2 with the tuned rules, then update the ocr/README.md tables, which still show the numbers from before the proposer rebuild. The re-read test books (Ars latine loquendi, Pro Patria, Kepler…) can then join Tier 3's four-way comparison, which today rests on two books.

Honest take: until this happens no number here can be quoted outside the repo. Cheap, and one-way — which is why step 3 goes first.

5

Speed: score several words per model call

Required for a harvesta day of engineering

The model is asked about one word at a time: ~4.7 s per 60-word passage on archive.org text, about two hours for a 100,000-word book.

Honest take: changes no result, so it was “conditional” before. With a harvest as the goal it is no longer optional — but do it after step 2 has shown there is enough to harvest.

6

A modern OCR engine for early print

Medium · uncertaindays · page images needed

archive.org's re-reading does little for books before 1700 (1495, 1619, 1658 above). Engines with models trained on early modern print exist — Kraken and Calamari are examples, as are vision-language models. Tier 2's early-print books (Kircher 1679, Kepler 1619, the 1533 Foenix) give a ready benchmark: same pages, proofread truth.

Honest take: the only lever measured or proposed here that could move 15% error rates on the neo-Latin that is actually new. Also the most open-ended; time-box it to one engine and three books first.

Medium valueone retrain

Retrain with the CSEL OCR text (5.85% of corpus-b) left out and rerun the tiers.

Honest take: tells you whether OCR-grade text helps the model at all. If leaving it out changes nothing, the case for harvesting more of it weakens — worth knowing before step 5's engineering.

Whole-page context

Speculativere-cut the benchmarks by page

The model's weakest spot is long-s (femen/semen); a human proofreader settles those by seeing the word printed correctly elsewhere on the page. The code for that clue exists; the 60-word passages starve it. A harvest works on whole pages anyway.

Honest take: the one idea aimed at the model's actual weakness. Unproven.

In one paragraph

Where it stands

The evaluation is sound and the scientific answer is in: reading context repairs more scanned Latin than counting words, at the same strict harm limit, and it holds on archive.org's text as it can be downloaded today — about a fifth of the damage repaired, about one correct word broken per thousand. But for the real goal, repair is the smallest lever. Nearly half of archive.org's Latin is already in the corpus; a fresh OCR reading removes far more error than repair does on anything printed in the last 150 years; and of the genuinely new Latin, the modern teaching texts come out clean while the early-modern prints stay poor whatever is done to them. What decides corpus quality now is a gate that keeps bad pages out, and a count of how much new, readable Latin there actually is.
